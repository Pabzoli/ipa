## ⭐ 1. The student loan "debt" that isn't really debt

Tom owes forty five grand in student loan. He hasn't checked the balance once in two years. And honestly? He shouldn't.

Here's the bit nobody explains when you graduate. Your student loan doesn't work like a normal loan. It's not on your credit file. It doesn't affect getting a mortgage. You pay 9% of whatever you earn over the threshold, every month, automatically, straight out your payslip — and after 30 to 40 years, whatever's left just gets wiped. Doesn't matter if it's forty grand or four hundred grand.

So for most graduates, the size of the balance is basically fiction. What actually matters is your monthly repayment, and that's fixed by your salary, not your debt.

Tom used to overpay it every bonus season thinking he was being responsible. He wasn't. He was just handing HMRC money he was never getting written off anyway. Now it goes into his pension instead.

## ⭐ 2. The pay rise that made him poorer

Tom got offered a pay rise. He said yes obviously. Six months later he worked out he was actually worse off. No, that's not a typo.

It's called the High Income Child Benefit Charge, and here's how it catches people out. Once your income goes over £60,000, you start losing your child benefit — 1% of it for every £200 you earn above that. By the time you hit £80,000, it's gone completely.

Tom's rise pushed him to £62,000. Didn't sound like a big deal. Except it triggered the charge, and he hadn't registered for self-assessment, so he ended up with a penalty on top for not reporting it.

The fix that actually helped him? Bumping up his pension contributions. That brings your income back down for this calculation, so it can rescue your child benefit AND grow your pension at the same time.

## 4. Your pension used to dodge this tax completely

For years, if you died with money left in your pension, your kids could inherit it completely free of inheritance tax. That's ending.

From April 2027, unused pension pots count towards your estate for inheritance tax. If your total estate — house, savings, pension, everything — goes over £325,000, anything above that gets taxed at 40%.

This isn't a "rich person" problem the way people assume. You don't need a seven figure pension. A normal house plus a normal pension can tip you over that line more easily than you'd think.

It doesn't touch state pension, and money left to a spouse or civil partner is still exempt. But if you're planning to leave your pension to your kids specifically, this is worth an actual conversation with an adviser before April 2027, not after.

## 5. The tax-free allowance that quietly dropped 75%

If you sold shares or a second property in 2022, the first £12,300 of profit was tax-free. Sell that exact same thing today? You only get £3,000 before HMRC wants a cut.

That's not a typo either. The capital gains allowance got cut from £12,300 down to £6,000, then down again to £3,000 — and it happened so gradually most people never clocked it.

It's also use it or lose it. Doesn't roll over to next year. If you're not using an ISA to shelter your investments, more of your gains are taxable now than at any point in over a decade.

Simple fix: if you're investing outside a pension or ISA, that £3,000 a year is basically the only shield you've got left, so timing when you sell actually matters now in a way it didn't before.

## ⭐ 6. Six years of overpaying, fixed in five minutes

Tom just found out he's been overpaying council tax for six years. The fix took him five minutes.

Every property in England and Scotland got put into a council tax band decades ago, back in 1991, based on what the house was worth THEN. A lot of those valuations were rough guesses. Nobody's ever gone back and double-checked most of them.

There's a free government tool that shows you what band your neighbours on your exact street are in. If you're in a higher band than houses that are basically identical to yours, that's your evidence to challenge it.

Tom challenged his, band came down, and he got backdated to when he moved in — call it a decent chunk of change for one form.

Fair warning though: it can occasionally go the other way and put your band UP, so only challenge if your neighbours are clearly lower than you, not just because you fancy a punt.

## ⭐ 8. HMRC owed them nine hundred quid and they had no idea

Tom and his wife had nine hundred pounds sitting with HMRC. Neither of them knew.

It's called Marriage Allowance. If one of you earns under £12,570 a year and the other's a normal basic rate taxpayer, the lower earner can transfer £1,260 of their tax-free allowance over to the higher earner. Saves up to £252 a year, every year, automatically, once you set it up.

The bit people miss — you can backdate it four years. Tom's wife had been a stay at home mum for three of those years and they'd never claimed once. Backdated it, current year included, and got over nine hundred pounds back in one payment.

Takes about ten minutes on the gov.uk site. Only the lower earner can apply, not the higher earner — do it the wrong way round and it just gets rejected.

Follow if you or your partner earns under £12,570.
